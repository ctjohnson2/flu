\begin{abstract}
Each year, influenza epidemics result in significant morbidity, mortality, and cause adverse effects to the economy. Predicting annual influenza outbreaks can prepare jurisdictions to reduce detrimental outcomes through targeted vaccinations, health care preparations, and other programs to reduce exposure. Since probability of exposure among individuals is enhanced through increased human density, we used incidence data from the Southern hemisphere during the winter to predict future influenza rates in the Northern hemisphere. The model predicted a 30\% increase in the winter of 2025 in North America which closely tracked actual incidence. We maintain models populated with data from other jurisdictions can be useful to predict relative incidence and be used to more effectively allocate resources from which to protect public health.  
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Annual influenza outbreaks cause considerable morbidity, mortality, and economic effects. There is also widespread concern amongst public health professionals regarding the deadly potential of a future influenza pandemic (Doshi 2008). Pathogenic airborne viruses increase transmission proportionally within higher areas of human density. Such increases occur annually during winter in temperate regions when people spend greater time indoors (Simonsen 1999). Tempo-spatial patterns can be useful in predicting influenza outbreaks (Onozuka and Akihito 2008, Shaman and Karspeck 2012) and can be used in modeling approaches to better prepare certain jurisdictions to focus public health resources.

\section{Southern Hemisphere Countries}



The nature of the Earth's tilt, with respect to its orbit around the Sun, provides a phase difference for the seasons of northern and southern hemisphere locations making winters $\sim\frac{1}{2}$ years apart. Given the ease people travel the globe, we assume the viral strains dominating the infections in the southern hemisphere will be similar to the strains dominating the northern hemisphere in the following season.


A country's size and latitude will generally dictate the significance of season change, so not every country in the southern hemisphere will have a winter to observe a flu season. We select a list of relevant ``candidates" by borrowing influenza records from the WHO and compute their lagged correlations with the U.S. hospitalizations records given by the CDC (specifically this is the flu-surv dataset which represents $\sim10\%$ of the population)\footnote{We could just as well have used WHO data as the target as well, but CDC hospitalizations were chosen to avoid bias from having the entire project built on a single data source.}. Specifically, we compute
\begin{equation}
C_{\alpha}(\delta t) =\frac{\sum_t(x^{US}_t - \bar{x}^{US})(x^{\alpha}_{t-\delta t}-\bar{x}^{\alpha})}{\sqrt{\sum_t(x^{US}_t - \bar{x}^{US})\sum_t(x^{\alpha}_{t-\delta t} - \bar{x}^{\alpha})}}
\end{equation}
where $x^{\alpha}_t$ represents the number of positive cases reported for country $\alpha$ on week $t$.

RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks) were once the state of the art for sequence learning commonly used in things like time-series forecasting and text sequence generation. They have lost popularity with the emergence of transformer models, but can still be preferable in small data situations with significant temporal dependancies. 

We employ RNNs to utilize feedback connections that enable them to learn sequences of data where ordering is important. These have the particular advantage of not needing a parametric description to capture the seasonality. The input features the 5 southern countries and the US influenza cases in batches of 26 week long sequences shifted by 21 weeks.

The architecture of the models follows the general recipe
\begin{equation}
o^t = \mathbf{B}_{\mu'\nu'}\mathbf{A}_{\mu'\nu',\mu\nu}\mathbf{i}^t_{\mu\nu}
\end{equation}
where the separable pieces $\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}$ are defined as 
\begin{align}
%\mathbf{A}^t_{\mu\nu} &=\left(\prod_{n} \mathbf{RNN}^{t,n}_{\mu'\nu'}\mathbf{ReLU}^{t,n}_{\mu''\nu'',\mu\nu''}\mathbf{Lin}^{t,n}_{\nu''\nu}\right)\\
\mathbf{A}_{\mu'\nu',\mu\nu} &\equiv  \mathbf{A}_{\mu_N'\nu_N',\mu_1\nu_1} = \left(\prod_{n=1}^N \mathbf{L}^{(n)}_{\nu_n'\nu_n''}\mathbf{\sigma}^{(n)}_{\mu_n'\nu_n'',\mu_n''\nu_n}\mathbf{R}^{(n)}_{\mu_n''\mu_n}\right)\\
\mathbf{B}_{\mu\nu} &=\mathbf{L}_{\gamma'''_N}\left(\prod_{n=1}^N\mathbf{L}_{\gamma_n'''\gamma_n''}^{(n)}\mathbf{D}(p)^{(n)}_{\gamma_n''\gamma_n'}\mathbf{\sigma}^{(n)}_{\gamma_n',\gamma_n}\right)\mathbf{F}_{\mu\cdot\nu\equiv \gamma} 
\end{align}
where $\mathbf{L}_{\gamma\gamma'}$ is a linear layer of shape $[\gamma,\gamma']$ (if only one index is included than it is assumed that $\gamma = 1$), $\mathbf{\sigma}$ is the activation function (generally RELU unless otherwise stated), $\mathbf{D}(p)$ is a dropout tensor with probability $p$, $\mathbf{F}$ is a flattening tensor, and $\mathbf{R}$ is the RNN where we make use of the slight different flavors of GRUs, LSTMs, and conventional RNN. The $\mathbf{A}$ piece of the model is inspired by the auto-encoder RNN design (cite here), with the intention of the first layer to encode the relevant features from the input from the candidates and the second RNN layer to act as the decoder. We include The $\mathbf{B}$ portion is designed to find the possible non-linear combinations of the individual countries that may best describe the U.S. flu season, as well as controlling over-fitting via the drop-out layers. This formulation allows for infinite model architectures to choose from and we explore a select few combinations creating an ensemble of models. 

This is an input tensor of shape $[T_{window},26,6]$ where $T_{window}$ is the total weeks included in the fitting procedure. The input tensor has a $21$-week lag in the time-sequenced index, so for a given week $t$, there will be $t-20,t-21,...,t-(20+26)$ sequenced data. This shift allows us to predict a data point that is 21 weeks away from the time the data is collected, and the sequence of 26 weeks is chosen such that is a sufficiently long enough window for the RNN to learn the pattern. Due to the nature of the normalization, insignificant pieces of the tensor will be nearly zero and will be suppressed compared to the peaks in the gradient when updating parameters.

Create an adjusted training metric that aims to capture most importantly the peak height, secondly location.

Training metric is 
\begin{equation}
MSE^{adj}= \sqrt{\left(y^{max}_{val} - y^{pred, max}_{val}\right)^2 + \alpha^2\left(\left(t^{max}_{val} - t^{pred, max}_{val}\right)^2 +MSE_{train}\right)}
\end{equation}

Training metric used to choose model is a hybrid of training MSE and how well the peak height and location are described in the validation season. The training MSE is included to ensure the model has trained sufficiently but is suppressed by a factor of $\alpha =0.1$ so that the peak height is still the dominant feature when searching for a minima or plateau in the training curve.

A simple average of the model variation will produce a poor description of the data due to the seasonality. One must first capture the phase differences and shift accordingly. A parametric solution to combining 
is the following sum of renormalized hybrid Lorentzian distribution
\begin{equation}
L(t) =  \sum_i A_i \frac{\left(\frac{\Gamma_i}{2}\right)^2}{(t-t_i)^2+\left(\frac{\Gamma_i}{2}\right)^2}
\end{equation}
where $A_i$ is the peak height, $t_i$ peak location, and $\Gamma_i$ is the half width. A typical Lorentzian has a defined normalization that we have removed to control the height. All three of the parameters can be computed for each model and their averages and deviations can be used to estimate the model variance.
Use a sum due to the double peak nature of some seasons.
